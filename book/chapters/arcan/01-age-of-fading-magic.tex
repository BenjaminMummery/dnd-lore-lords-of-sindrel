\chapter{The Age of Fading Magic}

\DndDropCapLine{F}{or most of recorded history}, magic was the dominant form of power on \gls{arcan}. Empires rose on the strength of their mage-lords; borders were drawn by spell and pact; \gls{thaumocracy}, rule by those who could reshape reality, was for many the ordinary condition of statecraft.

That age is ending.

Over the last few centuries, and with increasing speed in recent decades, the supernatural has been withdrawing from our world. What once seemed as permanent as stone now falters, dims, and fails. The argument is no longer \emph{whether} magic is dying, but how quickly, how completely, and what is to be done about it.

Already, the consequences are visible in every corner of the land. The decline of enchantment has reshaped politics, commerce, and faith. It has made the world smaller, more dangerous, and more uncertain. Entire generations have known only the slow, inexorable fading of the power that once defined our lives, thinking of the age of magic as a distant, almost mythical epoch.

\begin{DndQuote}{Unnamed merchant of the Brassmeade markets}[4215~SC]
  My grandfather's ring could light a hall for a century, and did. I inherited it in my twenty-third year. By my thirtieth the glow was fitful; by my thirty-fifth it was dead glass. The jeweller said he saw the like in every market from \gls{soulwind} to the Brassmeade. We are not poor craftsmen, he told me. The world is simply \emph{less} than it was.
\end{DndQuote}

The decline is not uniform. A village hedge-wizard may notice nothing for a lifetime while a court archmage finds her highest workings suddenly beyond reach. Yet the direction is consistent, and the testimony of those who work with enchantment daily has become difficult to dismiss.

\section{The Effects of the Fade}

\subsection{Spellcraft Weakens}

Mages who once reshaped battlefields now struggle to maintain simple illusions. Rituals that required a handful of adepts now demand circles of a dozen, and still fail. Summoning---always a delicate art---has grown treacherous: circles misbind, bindings slip, and entities that ought to be obedient arrive sullen or uncontrolled. In the great cities, such accidents are no longer rare curiosities but items of civic concern.

\subsection{Artefacts Exhaust}

Enchanted items that were made to last generations now spend their power in years, or months. Heirloom blades lose their edge of force; lamps of eternal flame gutter out; protective amulets crack and fall silent. Merchants who trade in magical goods speak of \emph{fresh stock} with the urgency once reserved for grain after a failed harvest.

\subsection{The Divine Grows Distant}

Those who draw power from \emph{the gods} report a withdrawal no less troubling than the failure of arcane craft. Prayers are answered slowly, faintly, or not at all. Priests who once spoke with authority now preach into a silence that their flocks find difficult to ignore. Whether this is cause, consequence, or coincidence of the arcane fade is fiercely disputed---a dispute that has itself become dangerous (see \emph{Responses to Decline}, below).

\subsection{The Land is Less Magical}

Creatures and peoples whose existence depended on ambient sorcery---fey-touched lineages, magically sustained ancestries, certain flora and fauna---find the world less hospitable. They drift toward whatever refuges still hold, or perish where they stand.

\section{When It Began}

Learned consensus places the first unmistakable stirrings of the fade around \textbf{3850 SC}---roughly three and a half centuries before the present year of \textbf{4218 SC}. For a long while the change was gradual enough to deny: a failed enchantment here, a shortened artefact there, easily blamed on sloppy craft or impiety. By \textbf{3900 SC}, denial was no longer tenable in the north. \gls{soulwind}, once a rival to any centre of magical learning on the continent, saw its population dwindle as its arts declined. Scholars mark that decade as the moment when the balance of power tipped decisively toward the one city that still seemed immune: \gls{sindrel}.

\begin{DndComment}{Margin note --- disputed chronology}
  Some Tellarin scholars (where such opinions can be obtained at all) insist the fade began earlier in the south. Northern chronicles treat this as typical southern gloom. Both agree only that the process has \emph{accelerated} within living memory.
\end{DndComment}

\section{Where Magic Still Holds}

\Gls{sindrel} is the great exception---so far as anyone on \gls{arcan} can verify, the last city where the \gls{death-of-magic} has not taken hold. Its walls bear impossible banners; its spires rise into structures that hang inverted in the air; airships glide between terraces with no visible means of support. In a world where the smallest illusion can be the talk of a county fair, Sindrel's expenditure of magical power is breathtakingly profligate. It has, naturally, become immensely wealthy and powerful by trading in the very goods and services that are vanishing everywhere else.

Why Sindrel alone remains vibrant, and how long that state of affairs can last, are questions to which no honest sage will give a certain answer. The houses publish reassurances; rival courts publish accusations. The traveller is advised to treat both as politics.

\textbf{Lesser refuges} exist. Pockets of old strength linger in private collections, buried vaults, and institutions that hoard what they cannot replace. Some successor states of the old \glslink{magedom-of-houd}{Houd} maintain a fraction of their former grandeur by rationing enchantment as a state resource. These are exceptions that prove the rule: the continent's baseline is decline; abundance is newsworthy.

Within Sindrel itself, three populations overlap. \emph{Native Sindrellians}---families whose lines have lived in the city for generations---were shielded from the fade long enough that their ancestry still reflects an older, more magical \gls{artresh}. \emph{Visiting dignitaries and traders} arrive wealthy, stay briefly, and depart; they resemble the mundane north of the present day. \emph{Refugees and the magically dispossessed} cluster in the lower tiers: tieflings, aasimar, genasi, and others who cannot make a life elsewhere as magic dies, and who have spent their last coin or credit on the journey north.

\section{Responses to Decline}

As old power structures crumble, new and ambitious ideologies compete for dominance. The chaos is growing, and the shape of the world is up for grabs.

\subsection{The Anathemists}

The most visible---and most violent---response is the rise of the \glspl{anathemist}. An inter-faith order that emerged in the northern reaches around \textbf{4185 SC}, they preach that mortal misuse of magic angered \emph{the gods}, who withdrew their grace and condemned \gls{arcan} to this slow death. Only by purging unholy sorcery from the face of the world, they hold, can divine favour be restored.

For decades they were fringe: street preachers, isolated cells, a nuisance on the margins of respectable theology. In the last few years, as the fade has quickened, they have grown bold. They probe \gls{mage-road}; they turn hostile to traffic bound for Sindrel; they have infiltrated the city itself. To an Anathemist, Sindrel is not a miracle but a blasphemy---a barrier to the gods' return, and therefore a target.

Since their escalation, many Sindrelians have grown wary of \emph{any} open religious practice, conflating devotion with anti-magical fanaticism. The Anathemists have succeeded, in part, by making faith itself suspect in the one place where faith was already unfashionable.

\subsection{Thaumocratic Retrenchment}

The old mage-lord class has not accepted obsolescence quietly. In the fractured north, petty kingdoms and city-states---the remnants of \glslink{magedom-of-houd}{Houd}---cling to privilege through marriage, alliance, feud, and occasional war. Their courts still speak the language of thaumocracy, but many rule in name more than in spell.

\textbf{3958 SC} marked a turning point: the \gls{seven-day-war} between Houd and the \gls{thaumocracy-of-tellerin}, driven in part by scarcity of magical resources. The Houdians were ultimately victorious, but the war drained what remained of imperial cohesion. The Magedom disintegrated into the patchwork of the present day. Tellarin, defeated, withdrew behind closed borders and has scarcely been seen since---a whole empire turned inward rather than adapt to a lesser age.

\subsection{The Sindrel Monopoly}

Where others retrench, Sindrel \emph{trades}. Its houses have parlayed near-exclusive access to reliable enchantment into a commercial empire spanning northern \gls{artresh} and, if rumour is credited, beyond Tellarin's sealed frontiers. Conjured food, airship freight, warded goods, and the labour of adepts who can still be adepts: these are the exports of a city that has made decline elsewhere into prosperity at home.

Critics call this hoarding dressed as commerce. \Glspl{factor} call it survival. The distinction matters little to the \gls{silver-guest-bracelet} visitor who arrives seeking a cure, a weapon, or a future---and finds that only Sindrel still sells such things at scale.

\subsection{Pragmatism and Panic}

Between the sermons and the spellbooks, ordinary people adapt. Caravans hire mercenaries where ward-stones once sufficed. Relic-brokers buy exhausted artefacts for scrap and story. Young people who would once have been sent to college are apprenticed to mundane trades. In the successor states, \textbf{4217 SC} saw even petty wars---the revolt against the so-called Mad King of \emph{Corvus}, mercenary companies hired to crush rebels---fought with steel and powder where once mages would have settled the matter in an afternoon.

The fade is not only a magical crisis. It is a political and economic one. Wars and revolutions grow more common as previously iron-clad structures seek to shore up crumbling positions. The world that emerges from this century will not resemble the world that entered it.
